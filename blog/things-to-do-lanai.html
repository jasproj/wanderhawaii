10 Things to Do on Lanai

Updated August 2026 · Lanai, Hawaii

Lanai is the smallest inhabited Hawaiian island you can visit, and that's exactly the point. No traffic lights. No chain restaurants. Just 3,000 residents, 30 miles of paved road, and a landscape that swings from pristine white-sand beaches to an otherworldly red-rock desert. Most of the 140-square-mile island is owned by Larry Ellison, and large stretches remain wild and untouched. Whether you're here for a day trip from Maui or a week at the Four Seasons, these are the ten things worth your time on Lanai.

Getting to Lanai: Most visitors take the Expeditions ferry from Lahaina Harbor on Maui — a 45-minute crossing that runs multiple times daily (about $30 each way). You can also fly from Honolulu on Mokulele Airlines in roughly 30 minutes. There's no public transit on the island, so rent a 4WD Jeep at the harbor or arrange a shuttle through the Four Seasons if you're a guest.

1. Hulopoe Bay

Hulopoe Bay is the one beach on Lanai that everyone can enjoy without a 4WD vehicle — and it's spectacular. This crescent of white sand sits inside a protected marine preserve, so the water is impossibly clear and the reef is thriving. Snorkel the left side of the bay where coral heads cluster close to shore and you'll find parrotfish, tangs, and moray eels tucked into crevices. The rocky tide pools on the southern edge are full of sea urchins and small crabs — perfect for kids. Look offshore in the mornings and you'll often spot pods of spinner dolphins arcing through the channel. Basic facilities are here: restrooms, outdoor showers, and shaded picnic tables. No vendors, no crowds. Just one of the best beaches in Hawaii, and most days you can count every person on it.

2. Garden of the Gods (Keahiakawelo)

About seven miles northwest of Lanai City, the pine forest gives way abruptly to something that looks like another planet. Keahiakawelo — the Garden of the Gods — is a windswept plateau of red, orange, and purple rock formations sculpted by centuries of erosion. Boulders balanced on pedestals. Spires jutting from bare earth. The colors shift dramatically at sunrise and sunset, making this one of the most photogenic spots in Hawaii. Hawaiian legend says the landscape was created during a spiritual contest between two kahuna from Lanai and Molokai. The road out is unpaved and rutted, so a 4WD vehicle is required. There are no guardrails or fences — just you and the rocks. Plan for 20 to 30 minutes of walking around the formations, plus the drive out and back.

3. Four Seasons Resort Lanai

Even if you're not staying here, the Four Seasons Lanai is part of the island's identity. Perched on the cliffs above Hulopoe Bay, it's the only major resort on Lanai and one of the most exclusive properties in Hawaii. The spa draws on traditional Hawaiian healing practices. The Manele Golf Course — an 18-hole Jack Nicklaus design — plays along ocean cliffs with views of Maui across the channel, and it's open to non-guests for a green fee. Several restaurants are on-site, from casual poolside dining to Nobu Lanai, one of the chef's few Hawaii outposts. Day visitors can book a spa treatment, a round of golf, or a meal without a room reservation. The grounds are beautifully maintained, the service is seamless, and the setting above the bay is hard to beat anywhere in the state.

4. Lanai Cat Sanctuary

This is the most unexpected attraction in Hawaii — and one of the most charming. The Lanai Cat Sanctuary is a three-acre outdoor refuge home to more than 600 free-roaming cats. It was founded to protect both the cats and Lanai's endangered native bird populations by giving the feral cats a permanent home with food, veterinary care, and human attention. Visitors can walk through the grounds, sit on benches while cats climb into their laps, and even adopt if they fall in love. The sanctuary is free to visit (donations welcome) and open daily. It sits just outside Lanai City, about a five-minute drive from town. Kids adore it. Adults who claim they're not cat people leave converted. It's a genuine feel-good stop that supports real conservation work.

5. Shipwreck Beach (Kaiolohia)

Lanai's north shore is eight miles of windblown sand, driftwood, and the rusting hull of a World War II-era Liberty Ship that ran aground on the reef and never left. Kaiolohia — Shipwreck Beach — is remote, dramatic, and not for swimming (the currents are strong and the bottom is rocky). It's for walking, exploring, and feeling genuinely far from anything. The drive in requires a 4WD vehicle on a rough dirt road. Once there, you can hike along the shoreline, photograph the shipwreck from the bluff, and look for ancient Hawaiian petroglyphs carved into the rocks at the eastern end of the beach. On clear days, the views across to Molokai are striking. Bring water, sunscreen, and a hat — there is zero shade. This is Lanai at its rawest.

6. Munro Trail

The Munro Trail is Lanai's premier hike — a ridgeline route that climbs through a Norfolk pine and Cook pine forest to the summit of Lanaihale at 3,370 feet. On a clear morning, the payoff is a panoramic view of five Hawaiian islands: Maui, Molokai, Oahu, the Big Island, and Kahoolawe. The trail runs about 12.8 miles as a full loop (most people drive the 4WD road partway and hike the final stretch to the summit), and the upper sections pass through genuine cloud forest where the air is cool and damp, moss covers every branch, and the contrast with the arid lowlands feels like entering a different island. Start early — clouds tend to roll in by late morning and erase the views. The road to the trailhead requires high clearance, and conditions deteriorate quickly after rain.

7. Lanai City

Lanai City is less a city and more a small plantation town that serves as the island's only real settlement. About 3,000 people live here, gathered around Dole Park — a central green square shaded by Cook pines that dates to Lanai's days as the world's largest pineapple plantation. The town is walkable in fifteen minutes. Browse the local shops around the park for Hawaiian art, jewelry, and Lanai-made goods. Grab a plate lunch at Blue Ginger Cafe, pick up snacks for a day of exploring at Pine Isle Market, or get coffee at Coffee Works. On weekends the small farmers market sets up near the park. There's a quiet, genuine community feel here that you won't find in any resort area in Hawaii. It's worth at least an hour of wandering.

8. Snorkeling and Diving

Lanai's underwater terrain is among the best in Hawaii, and it sees a fraction of the divers and snorkelers that Maui's sites do. The star attractions are the Cathedrals — two lava-tube cavern systems off the south shore where shafts of sunlight pierce through openings in the rock and illuminate the chambers below. First Cathedral, the more famous of the two, feels like swimming through a flooded church. Spinner dolphins are regular companions in the channel between Lanai and Maui, and manta ray sightings happen on night dives during summer months. Most dive and snorkel charters depart from Lahaina Harbor on Maui and include the crossing as part of the trip. Hulopoe Bay is the best shore-snorkeling option, with easy entry and consistent visibility.

9. Off-Road Exploring

Lanai has only 30 miles of paved road, which means the rest of the island is a network of red-dirt 4WD tracks that lead to places most visitors never see. Polihua Beach on the far northwest coast is one of the longest white-sand beaches in Hawaii — completely deserted, wind-scoured, and occasionally visited by Hawaiian monk seals and green sea turtles during nesting season. The Naha trail on the eastern coast follows an ancient Hawaiian path through coastal scrub to rocky shoreline and tide pools. Getting to either requires a capable 4WD vehicle, a full tank of gas, and some patience with the road conditions. There are no services, no cell signal, and no other people. That's the appeal. Rent a Jeep from the harbor or in Lanai City and budget a full day for exploring.

10. Day Trip from Maui

For many visitors, the smartest way to experience Lanai is as a day trip from Maui. The Expeditions ferry departs Lahaina Harbor multiple times daily, and the 45-minute crossing through the Auau Channel often includes whale sightings in winter and dolphin encounters year-round. Book the earliest departure, rent a 4WD at the Manele Harbor, and you can fit in Hulopoe Bay, the Cat Sanctuary, Garden of the Gods, and lunch in Lanai City before the last ferry back. It's one of the best day trips in all of Hawaii — you leave the crowds behind completely. If you're staying on Maui's west side, check out our guide to things to do in Lahaina to plan the rest of your day around the ferry schedule.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you get to Lanai from Maui?

The easiest way to reach Lanai is the Expeditions ferry from Lahaina Harbor on Maui. The crossing takes about 45 minutes and runs multiple times daily. You can also fly from Honolulu on a short puddle-jumper flight with Mokulele Airlines, which takes roughly 30 minutes.

Do you need a 4WD vehicle on Lanai?

Yes, a 4WD vehicle is essential if you want to explore beyond Lanai City and Hulopoe Bay. Most of the island's best attractions — including Garden of the Gods, Shipwreck Beach, and Polihua Beach — are only accessible via unpaved red-dirt roads that require high clearance and four-wheel drive.

Is Lanai worth visiting for a day trip?

Absolutely. A day trip from Maui gives you enough time to explore Hulopoe Bay, visit Lanai City for lunch, and stop at the Lanai Cat Sanctuary. If you rent a 4WD vehicle at the harbor, you can also reach Garden of the Gods. Many visitors find the quiet, uncrowded feel of Lanai a refreshing contrast to Maui.

What is the best beach on Lanai?

Hulopoe Bay is Lanai's best and most accessible beach. It's a protected marine preserve with calm, clear water ideal for snorkeling, tide pools along the rocky edges, and frequent spinner dolphin sightings offshore. It's also the only beach on the island with facilities like restrooms and picnic areas.
