Mauna Kea: The World's Tallest Mountain and Hawaii's Star Country

Updated October 2026 • 7 min read

Mauna Kea's summit stands 13,796 feet above sea level — high enough for snow in winter, high enough to rise above the clouds, high enough that the air at the top contains roughly 40 percent less oxygen than at sea level. But measured differently, from its base on the floor of the Pacific Ocean to its summit, Mauna Kea rises more than 33,500 feet — more than 4,000 feet taller than Mount Everest measured base-to-peak. By this metric, Mauna Kea is the tallest mountain on Earth.

The Guinness World Records selects a base in the Hawaiian Trough and reports a total height of approximately 33,480 feet (10,205 meters). The majority of that mountain is below sea level, invisible, holding up the island of Hawaii. Only the top 13,796 feet poke above the water. But the mountain's scale is real regardless of where you start measuring — it is a volcano of extraordinary size, built layer by layer over hundreds of thousands of years, and it sits in the middle of the Pacific Ocean with nothing tall for thousands of miles in any direction.

The Summit: The World's Best Place to Watch the Universe

Thirteen telescopes from 11 countries operate on Mauna Kea's summit — the highest concentration of world-class astronomical observatories on Earth. The combination of factors that make the summit extraordinary for astronomy is rare: altitude that puts it above the cloud layer and above most of Earth's atmospheric water vapor; dry, stable air that minimizes the atmospheric turbulence that blurs telescope images; more than 300 clear nights per year; and very little light pollution.

The Keck Observatory, home to the twin Keck telescopes with 33-foot primary mirrors, has been one of the most productive astronomical research facilities on Earth since its opening in the 1990s. Mauna Kea instruments have contributed to discoveries about black holes, the accelerating expansion of the universe, exoplanet atmospheres, and the structure of distant galaxies. Astronomers describe the site as having produced more significant discoveries per telescope than any other ground-based observatory location on Earth.

The observatories' domes, visible from the summit parking area and from far below along the road, are a striking visual — white and silver spheres sitting on red volcanic cinder at nearly 14,000 feet, framed by blue sky above and clouds below. Nothing about the landscape looks like you're still on a tropical island. It looks like the moon.

Acclimatization warning: The summit at 13,796 feet has roughly 40% less oxygen than sea level. Altitude sickness — headache, nausea, dizziness — is common. Do not drive directly from sea level to the summit without stopping at the Visitor Information Station (VIS) at 9,200 feet for at least 30 minutes. Children under 13, pregnant women, and people with heart or respiratory conditions should stay at the VIS rather than ascending to the summit. Drink plenty of water.

Mauna a Wākea: The Sacred Mountain

In Hawaiian tradition, the mountain is called Mauna a Wākea — "Mountain of Wākea." Wākea is the sky father, one of the primordial ancestral figures in Hawaiian cosmology, whose union with Papahānaumoku (earth mother) is understood to have produced the Hawaiian Islands and, through the lineage, the Hawaiian people. Mauna Kea is considered Wākea's firstborn — the highest, most sacred point of the islands, where the earth reaches closest to the heavens.

The summit was used for traditional ceremonies and as a burial place for high-ranking ali'i. Ancient shrines and burial sites exist near the summit, some of which were disturbed during early telescope construction. The debate over the Thirty Meter Telescope (TMT) project, which would be the largest telescope on Mauna Kea if built, has brought international attention to the conflict between the summit's scientific value and its profound cultural significance to Native Hawaiians. The conversation is ongoing and unresolved.

Stargazing from the Visitor Information Station

Most visitors who want to experience Mauna Kea's exceptional skies don't need to go to the summit — the Visitor Information Station at 9,200 feet offers outstanding stargazing and is far safer and more comfortable for most people. The VIS runs free public stargazing programs on most clear nights, with staff astronomers pointing out constellations, planets, and deep-sky objects through telescopes set up outside.

At 9,200 feet, the sky is already dramatically darker than at sea level, and on clear nights — particularly from October through April when clouds are least frequent — the Milky Way is visible to the naked eye. The Southern Cross is visible from Hawaii at this latitude, a reminder of how far south the Big Island sits compared to the rest of the United States.

Snow on a Tropical Mountain

Mauna Kea receives snow at its summit, sometimes in significant amounts. The mountain can be covered in white from November through March, occasionally longer. The image of a snow-capped peak rising above tropical beaches a few miles away is one of the more surreal aspects of the Big Island. From the Kona coast on a clear winter day, you can sometimes see the white summit glinting in the sun while standing in warm sand at sea level.

Locals ski and snowboard on the summit in winter — there are no lifts, so it's a hike-up operation, but it is a real thing that happens. Surfing and skiing in the same day on the same island is not a common boast, but it is technically achievable on the Big Island in the right conditions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Mauna Kea really taller than Mount Everest?

By one measure, yes. Everest is highest above sea level at 29,032 feet. Mauna Kea's summit is 13,796 feet above sea level. But Mauna Kea rises from the ocean floor — measured base-to-peak, it totals more than 33,500 feet, making it Earth's tallest mountain by that metric.

Why is Mauna Kea the best place for astronomy?

Its summit sits above roughly 40% of Earth's atmosphere and 98% of its water vapor, providing cleaner, clearer views than almost any other ground-based site. Combined with dark skies, stable atmospheric conditions, and 300+ clear nights per year, it hosts 13 telescopes from 11 countries and has produced more major discoveries than any other observatory site on Earth.

Is Mauna Kea sacred to Native Hawaiians?

Yes — it is considered the most sacred site in Hawaiian culture. In Hawaiian tradition, Mauna Kea (Mauna a Wākea) is the firstborn of the sky father Wākea, an ancestor of the Hawaiian people. The summit was used for ceremonies and burials. This cultural significance is at the heart of ongoing debates about telescope development on the mountain.
